# Adoption best practices

The human layer over a company brain. The other two documents in this folder
define what the system has to *be* — a real context graph, a real control
plane. This one defines what the people around it have to *do*, because
across Day AI workspaces the build decides the ceiling and behavior decides
the outcome.

The pattern shows up most clearly in pairs. Two workspaces of the same shape,
on the same product, with the same stock briefings landing on the same
schedule. In one, people start several live conversations per person per
week and the number keeps climbing; in the other, a fraction of one, while
the scheduled deliveries keep arriving on time to a team that has stopped
reading them. Same product, same fit. The difference is everything below.

Ten practices. Each pairs a diagnostic question (ask it of any company brain,
including your own) with why it matters, what good looks like, and what we
have seen. Used by `day-ai/company-brain-evaluation` as the third audit lens for
`COMPANY-BRAIN-UPGRADE.md` — the one that decides whether the first two
lenses ever matter.

One caveat to hold onto while reading: these are patterns observed across
live workspaces, not a controlled experiment. Where a habit and heavy use
appear together, "the habit drives the use" and "teams that use it grow the
habit" are not fully separable. Read the ratios as the shape of the effect.

---

## 1. Fit is the floor: the brain serves a conversation-shaped deal motion

**Ask:** *How many open deals does your team hold in its head at once, and
where do those deals actually live?*

**Why it matters:** a company brain is an answer to overload. It earns its
keep when there are more live deals than people can track, when the deals
live in calls, threads, and channels rather than fields, when a standing
meeting already reviews them, and when the calendar applies pressure — dated
closes, quarterly targets, a board cadence. Absent that shape, the best build
in the world has nothing to do each morning.

**What good looks like:** before scoring the architecture, score the motion.
Seven traits, each 0–2, all visible before anything is built: deal overload;
deals live in conversations; a standing pipeline ritual; a builder in the
building; process younger than ambition; resident AI power users; calendar
pressure. High scores justify a serious build. Low scores mean the plan should
say so — a founder's personal daily workflow, not a fleet.

**What we have seen:** when these traits are scored on a company's business
alone, without looking at how it uses the product, the scores line up with
engagement anyway — on every trait individually, not just the composite. The
teams that did not stay scored well below even the lightest active users.
Four shapes reliably do not work: a few huge episodic deals with no daily
"what changed?"; work that isn't conversation-shaped (production, casework,
deliverables the graph never sees); organizations structurally bound to an
incumbent system; and mid-size teams with seats but neither founder intensity
nor a builder. Habits cannot rescue a bad fit — poor-fit teams that built the
leadership ritual below still did not become heavy users. Fit is the floor;
everything else here is the multiplier.

## 2. Someone builds — and builds for the team, not for themselves

**Ask:** *Who besides you has written a skill in the last three weeks, and
who did they write it for?*

**Why it matters:** the single strongest structural predictor of a live
workspace is an active builder. But the sharpest lesson is the builder who
builds only for himself: a handful of genuinely good custom skills, every one
on his own agent, still firing on schedule while the rest of the team never
picks the product up. A one-hero rig is the DIY default, and it is also the
most common way a well-fit team goes flat. In the pairs above, both
workspaces had a builder — the difference was reach.

**What good looks like:** a builder with a mandate to build for everyone.
Workspace-level programs every agent inherits, as opposed to something one
person set up for themselves. The test is simple: count the skills and
briefings that exist for people other than their author.

**What we have seen:** workspaces with both an active power user (someone who
created a custom skill within the last three weeks) and a builder-titled
agent become high adopters roughly nine times as often as workspaces with
neither. A builder *title* without an active power user is mostly
low-adopting — the org-chart label means nothing unless the person actually
builds. In the pair above: one workspace had dozens of self-written skills
and dozens of team-wide scheduled programs; the other had none of either, and
everything it ran was stock or personal.

## 3. Ops owns the build; reps tune on top

**Ask:** *If the builder left tomorrow, does the system survive the month?*

**Why it matters:** salespeople will not maintain skills, and they shouldn't
have to. Where a build survived contact with a busy sales team, it was
because the core lift of getting skills set up and maintained sat with ops
and enablement, and reps tuned their own copies on top. That is the
governance dial (control plane, practice 8) applied to people: managed
skills owned by the function, personal drift owned by the individual. It is
also the argument against heroics — when the builder leaves, an un-owned
system leaves with them.

**What good looks like:** an ops or enablement owner of record for the
managed layer; every rep with their own named agent they can coach without a
ticket; a rule for what is invariant (forecast methodology, CRM writeback,
qualification framework) and what is personal (tone, timing, format).

**What we have seen:** in the workspace that stuck, ops built and ran a named
deal coach for every rep — dozens of agents, none left on the generic setup —
and reps tuned on top. The clearest way a well-fit, well-built workspace goes
quiet is the builder changing roles or leaving with nobody named to own what
they built.

## 4. Every briefing feeds a meeting that already exists

**Ask:** *Which standing meeting on your team's calendar runs on the brain's
output? What breaks in that meeting if the briefing doesn't arrive?*

**Why it matters:** this is the ignition event — the moment a company brain
stops being a tool and becomes part of how the team operates. It is never
created; it is attached. The brain plugs into a meeting the company already
runs, and the briefing lands shortly before it. Where this happens, dead
workspaces become rare. Where it doesn't, beautiful builds go flat with
nothing wrong in them.

**What good looks like:** every scheduled output has a named meeting it
serves — the weekly deal review, the forecast call, the Monday pipeline
standup, the 1:1 — and a delivery time set relative to that meeting, in the
channel where the attendees already are. Reps update the brain *because* the
meeting will expose them if they don't. The briefing is the price of
admission to a meeting they were going to attend anyway.

**What we have seen:** where the leader quotes agent numbers and the team's
meetings visibly run on the brain, a workspace is about three times less
likely to end up barely used than where neither is true. It is also the step
most teams never take — the majority of workspaces never form the ritual,
which is why it is written down here. One caution before reading any quiet
workspace as proof: check all three usage surfaces first (practice 7). More
than once a fleet that looked "built but never ignited" on chat turned out to
be doing its work over a programmatic interface instead.

## 5. The leader's number comes out of the agent

**Ask:** *When your leader quotes a pipeline number, where did it come from?*

**Why it matters:** the active ingredient in practice 4 is the leader, not
the meeting. We have not seen a team cadence form on the brain unless the
leader consumed agent output first. When the person the team answers to runs
their week off an agent's numbers, the team follows — because the briefing
is now the thing the boss is reading. This is also where the
leadership-visibility use case earns its priority: if nothing reports upward,
there is nothing for a leadership habit to form around.

**What good looks like:** a "Monday number" — team scorecard, pipeline
delta, deal-approval surface — delivered to the leader before their operating
meeting, and cited by them in it. Commit and forecast figures sourced to a
brain thread. Deal approvals verified against the brain's read of the
qualification methodology. The leader's numbers are agent output, and everyone
knows it.

**What we have seen:** the leader habit alone, before any team ritual forms,
carries most of the effect. For teams of middling fit it is the biggest
swing anywhere — with the habit, they engage like strong-fit teams; without
it, they don't. In the workspace that stuck, leadership prep, the weekly deal
review, and methodology checks on every approval all come out of agents, and
the commit figure quoted internally is sourced to a brain thread. In
workspaces that went flat, the upward-reporting briefing is the one most
often found scoped and never shipped.

## 6. Rewrite the defaults; nothing generic survives

**Ask:** *How many of your briefings were written for your methodology, by
name? How many are still the stock prompt?*

**Why it matters:** stock briefings deliver everywhere and ignite nowhere. A
briefing nobody tailored teaches people to ignore the channel it arrives on —
and it keeps arriving, on time, long after anyone stopped reading. A generic
default is not neutral; it actively trains non-adoption. This is practice 9
of the control plane (definitions authored from the company's own history)
seen from the adoption side: the authoring matters because the generic
version does harm.

**What good looks like:** every scheduled skill rewritten for the team's
actual methodology, roles, and data — the qualification framework by name,
the real stage names, the real 1:1 cadence. Defaults treated as scaffolding
to be replaced in the first two weeks, not as the product. If a briefing has
not been rewritten, the question is whether it should run at all.

**What we have seen:** nearly every workspace receives the stock briefings,
and receiving them does not by itself move engagement. Five or more
briefings a human actually wrote for the team is associated with about four
times the adoption. A builder and a power user *without* self-written
briefings roughly halves the odds of a heavy-use workspace. The workspace that
stuck replaced every stock briefing with its own; a common shape among
well-fit but quiet workspaces is a dozen identical, untouched copies of the
stock daily-schedule prompt spread across many agents.

## 7. Delivery is near-zero-UI — and delivery is not adoption

**Ask:** *Do people have to log in to get value? And do they talk back —
how many conversations did your team start last week, versus how many
briefings you sent?*

**Why it matters:** two lessons in one. First, if reps must open a web app,
adoption dies; the workspaces that stuck made channel-first, near-zero-UI
delivery an explicit requirement after prior tools failed exactly there.
Second, and more dangerous: a machine that delivers on schedule looks alive
from the outside. A workspace can send a thousand briefings in three weeks
while its people open two dozen conversations. Counting sends would call it
healthy.

**What good looks like:** output arrives where people already work (Slack DM,
channel, email) and replies are a first-class path back. Adoption is measured
as conversations people start and replies they send — user-initiated threads,
not deliveries. The full usage picture is chat plus asynchronous delivery
plus programmatic access over MCP, because some teams legitimately consume
the brain through integrations and digests with little chat; those need a
delivery-health measure, not activation nagging. Read receipts and delivery
health are retention infrastructure.

**What we have seen:** heavy-use workspaces run several live conversations
per person per week; flat ones run a fraction of one with their scheduled
briefings still landing daily. Separately, teams that look quiet on chat
sometimes turn out to be among the heaviest programmatic users of the graph.
Two non-chat patterns are legitimate value states, not failure modes: the
digest-ambient team, which pays for ingestion and digests and whose health is
delivery and read health; and the integration-primary team, which drives the
brain from its own tools and whose health is session health — and which ends
abruptly on breakage, so it needs a breakage alert, not a decline alert. The
rule: three surfaces quiet at once is the signal; one surface quiet is a
channel question. Grade on all three or grade wrong.

## 8. Coverage beats polish

**Ask:** *Which of your team's recurring jobs has no skill behind it?* Walk
the list: post-call coaching, per-deal coaching, pre-meeting prep, inbound
lead handling, prospecting, RFP and bid work, pipeline hygiene, upward
reporting.

**Why it matters:** the workspaces that stick are not flawless — duplicate
triggers firing on the same email, imports that never finished
configuration. Workspaces that go flat sometimes contain a single genuinely
elegant build. The difference is not quality. It is that a call-heavy sales
team never got a call-coaching use case, prep and RFP skills were scoped and
never shipped, pipeline automation was tried and switched off with nothing
replacing it, and nothing reported upward. Each uncovered job is a place the
team still works the old way, and the old way wins by default.

**What good looks like:** a use-case coverage map, drawn against the team's
actual week, with a build (even a rough one) behind each recurring job and
the upward-reporting row filled first (practice 5). Breadth of coverage over
depth on one showcase.

**What we have seen:** in the pair above, rep coaching ran to dozens of
instances in one workspace and a single debrief skill on one agent in the
other; manager and leadership visibility ran to ten-plus skills reporting up
in one and none in the other. Everything the quiet workspace lacked had been
scoped at some point. The pattern is not ignorance of the use cases; it is
never shipping them.

## 9. The rollout has dates and a launch moment

**Ask:** *What is the date of your full-team launch? What does each side owe
the other before it?*

**Why it matters:** workspaces do not grow into engagement. Tenure predicts
nothing — high adopters ignite early or not at all. That makes the first two
weeks the whole game and argues for a dated crawl → walk → run with a real
launch on the calendar, rather than a soft "we'll roll it out as people pick
it up." A pilot with no end date is a pilot with no decision.

**What good looks like:** a committed pilot with an end date; a walk phase
where both sides owe dated deliverables; a run launch tied to an event that
already exists (a kickoff, a team gathering); and the success criterion for
the whole rollout stated as a behavior, not an inventory: *the leader runs
their week off an agent briefing within two weeks.* Not "skills deployed."

**What we have seen:** the workspace that stuck ran a dated pilot, a walk
phase with mutual deliverables, and an in-person launch at an event the team
already had on its calendar. A workspace a month old can have its CEO citing
a named agent's briefing for his own pipeline number in week four.
Workspaces with long tenure and no ritual sit flat for months — time does not
help them.

## 10. The record makes its own case

**Ask:** *Six months in, which system does your team open first when they
want to know what is true about a deal?*

**Why it matters:** where a legacy CRM exists, the brain and the CRM run in
parallel, and that is the right sequence: the brain maintains the CRM from
what actually happened (control plane, practice 10), the team keeps the
reports it already trusts, and nobody is asked to give anything up. But
parallel is a starting condition, not a steady state. Over time one of the
two becomes the place people look, and the plan should be built so the brain
can win that comparison on merit — full-resolution history, provenance on
every value, the numbers leadership already quotes (practice 5) — rather than
by decree. The team reaches its own conclusion, in its own time, with the
evidence in front of it.

**What good looks like:** the bridge in place early — the CRM connected and
kept current by agents before the build scales — and no forced migration
anywhere in the plan. Instead, a way of noticing: which system the deal
review actually runs on, where the forecast number is sourced, whether the
"update CRM" calendar blocks have gone to zero. The document records what the
team would want to be true before it revisits the question, and leaves the
question to them.

**What we have seen:** the workspace that stuck connected its CRM early in
the walk phase and let the build scale on top of it; its leadership numbers
now come out of the brain while the CRM stays connected. Among teams that
never had a CRM, more than one founder refers to their agent, by name, as
"the CRM." The pattern is convergence by use, not by mandate.

## Three tripwires, weekly

Practices are what to build toward. These are what to watch for once it is
running — each one is catchable months before it costs the team the system.
Read all three usage surfaces (practice 7) before acting on any of them:

1. **Usage falls during the honeymoon.** Consolidating onto the brain is a
   behavior change, and engagement can dip sharply in the weeks right after
   it. Watch that window deliberately.
2. **The champion is not a member.** An executive sponsor who advocates but
   holds no seat and has built nothing. Advocacy without use is not
   ownership.
3. **The solo builder.** Skills accumulating on one person's agent and nowhere
   else. Team-ify them before the workspace decays, not after.

## Where each practice lands in `company-brain-evaluation`

- **Phase 1 (take stock):** practices 1–3 are readable from the folder and
  from `git shortlog` — the fit traits, who has written skills for whom, who
  owns what. A one-committer repo is practice 2's warning sign.
- **Phase 2 (the goal):** practice 1 refines the persona split. The two ends
  of the barbell are the strong fits: a founder under ~20 people who sells,
  and a 50+ team with a titled, *active* ops builder. The middle — seats, no
  founder intensity, no builder — needs an interim builder named in the plan
  or an honest scaled-down scope.
- **Phase 3 (the document):** practices 4–10 belong in the upgrade plan
  itself. Section 7 should name the meeting, the leader, the launch date, and
  the CRM bridge. Section 4 (the bar) should read Adoption as this
  document reads it: the whole team's use, measured as conversations started,
  attached to a standing ritual.
- **Phase 5 (`implement-upgrade/`):** the first implementation initiative is the
  ignition event — the leader's Monday number on top of whatever data lands
  first. Everything else follows it.
